The MJRTY Effect

We do not claim that Yelp's system is biased. Instead, we demonstrate how a seemingly reasonable filtering and aggregation pipeline can produce systematic bias under certain conditions. Using a model inspired by the Boyer–Moore majority vote algorithm, we show how minority signals can be gradually suppressed, compounded over time, and transformed into ratings that appear objective — but reflect structure, not quality.

Section 02

A MJRTY-Like Cancellation Effect

In streaming algorithms like Boyer-Moore MJRTY, minority elements are cancelled one-for-one by a dominant majority until they disappear from the result. Our question: can a similar dynamic emerge in rating aggregation? In this model, if majority reviewers score minority cuisines lower, their dominance in the pool produces a cancellation effect. Every restaurant below has the same true quality: 4.0★. This is not how any specific platform explicitly works — it illustrates a class of aggregation dynamics worth examining.

Section 03

The Spiral: Bias Compounds Without Correction

The effect in Section 02 does not stay constant — under this model's assumptions, it compounds. Lower ratings reduce visibility, which reduces reviews from minority diners, which skews the pool further, which lowers ratings further still. No single step is malicious. The system only needs to be uncorrected. This is the core risk of streaming aggregation without a fairness constraint.

The algorithm does not need to be malicious. It only needs to be uncorrected.

The unfairness does not require any single bad design decision — it emerges from the interaction of individually reasonable components: a quality filter, a weighted average, a ranking function. Without an explicit correction mechanism, the structure does the rest.

"The Company's orders do not differ from those lavished by impostors."

— Jorge Luis Borges, The Lottery in Babylon

In Borges' story, the Lottery begins as a simple game and gradually takes over all of civic life — each step individually justifiable, the final state indistinguishable from chaos, yet defended as legitimate process. Rating systems can follow a similar arc: quality filtering is reasonable, weighted averaging is reasonable, ranking is reasonable. But a chain of reasonable steps can produce a structurally biased outcome — and the output number hides the chain entirely. The star rating looks like it came from quality. Under these conditions, it came from structure.

Section 05

What This Model Shows

This simulation illustrates how unfairness can emerge — not from any single malicious design decision, but from the interaction of reasonable components: a quality filter, a preference-weighted aggregate, a feedback loop, a number. The concern is not a specific platform. It is a general pattern: filter → aggregation → feedback → compression.

In this model, restaurants don't rank lower because they are worse. They rank lower because their advocates were filtered out, their positive signals cancelled, the gap compounded without correction, and a decimal was placed over the result to make it look clean. Whether any real system exhibits this pattern is an empirical question — and one worth asking.

What systems like this claim

Objective rules applied equally to all. Quality signals surface the best results. The number reflects reality.

What this model reveals

Under correlated-preference conditions, quality filtering concentrates weight in a majority pool. Aggregation then cancels minority signals. Feedback compounds the gap. The output number buries all three steps.
